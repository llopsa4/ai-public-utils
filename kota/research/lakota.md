## Homeland and landscapes (for home-region rooms)

- Tetonwan (Lakota) bands: Hunkpapa, Sicangu/Brule, Itazipo/Sans Arc, Sihasapa, Oglala, Oohenumpa, Minniconjou, with present reservations such as Standing Rock, Rosebud, Lower Brule, Cheyenne River, Pine Ridge. https://aktalakota.stjo.org/oceti-sakowin-seven-council-fires/ (corroborate).
- Lakota/Dakota were once woodland people east of the Missouri; warfare and the horse moved them onto the northern plains. https://www.ndstudies.gov/curriculum/high-school/standing-rock-oyate/culture-standing-rock (corroborate).
- Emergence story is set at Wind Cave (Black Hills): the natural entrance is a small hole about ten inches wide in a rocky outcrop; bison on prairie and grassland are central. https://www.nps.gov/wica/learn/historyculture/the-lakota-emergence-story.htm
- Badlands: Lakota name mako sica, "bad lands"; wet clay is slick and sticky, jagged canyons and buttes, few muddy water sources, cold windy winters and hot dry summers. https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/mako-sica.htm . Badlands South Unit is jointly administered with the Oglala Sioux Tribe; mixed-grass prairie; bison present (NPS, via search summary, not opened).
- Camp sites were chosen partly for closeness to buffalo herds. https://www.ndstudies.gov/curriculum/high-school/standing-rock-oyate/culture-standing-rock
- Oglala leaders state they have never relinquished their claim to the entire Great Sioux Reservation including the Black Hills (He Sapa). https://www.wakpamnilake-nsn.gov/ (see source 11; search summary only). Treat the Black Hills as a place of living significance; see Restricted section.
- Room ideas supported by sources only: prairie with bison herd, eroded Badlands buttes and clay, a narrow cave mouth leading down, a hill above a flooded plain (from Lame Deer story). No source yet for woods, rivers or specific plants; see Open questions.

## Publicly told stories with defeatable monsters (story, monster, how it is defeated, whether the story is publicly told/published; ONLY candidates that are clearly NOT sacred beings honoured today)

Honest finding: I found no Lakota-sourced text in this pass that clearly shows a monster being defeated. The candidates below are either cross-nation or unread.

1. **Iktomi conquers Iya, the eater** (Ella Deloria, *Dakota Texts*, 1932). Monster: Iya, who eats people (title of the story only; I did not read it). Defeat: title says Iktomi conquers it; a second-hand search summary says Iktomi tricks Iya into eating him, then cuts it open and frees everyone (that summary came from encyclopedic sites, not the text; unverified). Published: yes (Deloria, 1932). Source: https://muse.jhu.edu/article/531633/summary . Doubtful: unread; Deloria's collection mixes Dakota and Teton material, so check whether this text is Lakota; the Iya of other tellings may be a sacred being (see Restricted). Needs a read of the text.
2. **The Badger and the Bear** (Zitkala-Sa, Dakota, 1901). Monster: a large black bear who takes over a badger family's home and keeps their food and arrows. Defeat: a magic-arrow "Dakota avenger" confronts the bear, who flees; the bear is driven off, not killed. The bear's youngest cub secretly feeds the badgers. Published: yes. Source: https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/zitkala-sa/old-indian-legends/text/single-page . Fits the "released, not taken" idea well. Cross-nation (Dakota); needs the user's decision.
3. **The Tree-Bound** (Zitkala-Sa, Dakota, 1901). Monster: a giant red eagle that swoops out of a chalk bluff each morning and terrorises a village; a Dakota avenger with a magic arrow goes against it. Ending not read (text cut off in my fetch). Published: yes. Same source. Caution: eagles are sacred in current Lakota practice; a "giant red eagle" monster may be a poor fit. Do not use without review.
4. **Iktomi and the Handkerchief / Pointing Game / Prairie Chickens / Animal Warrior Society** (Duane Hollow Horn Bear, video recordings). Iktomi is a trickster who is himself fooled or beaten (e.g. loses a pointing contest to a branch; is crushed by a rock he took from lizard children). He is a self-defeating trickster, not a monster; "defeat" is by consequence. Publicly told: yes, recorded and published. Source: https://www.wolakotaproject.org/storytelling-with-duane-hollow-horn-bear/ . Possible use: Iktomi as a mischief-making foe whose tricks backfire. Caution: Iktomi's status (sacred or not) is not settled in my sources; see Open questions.

Not a candidate: Unktehi (see Restricted).

## Helper figures (animal relatives / elders who help in stories; flag any that are sacred)

- **Mother meadowlark**: outwits a snake by keeping it talking while she escapes with her youngest chick. Public recording. https://www.wolakotaproject.org/storytelling-with-duane-hollow-horn-bear/
- **Coyote**, **turtle, turkey, dragonfly, skunk** (the animals Iktomi recruits; skunk's defence ends the battle): appear in the same recordings (same source). Skunk's defence is a usable defensive-skill motif.
- **Grandfather figure** warns Iktomi (ignored) in the prairie-chicken series (same source). An elder who gives sound advice.
- **Wolf**: Anog-Ite's companion (Sungmanitu Tanka) is harmful in the emergence story, but a wolf also leads people up to the surface; role is ambivalent. https://www.nps.gov/wica/learn/historyculture/the-lakota-emergence-story.htm
- **Bison**: the Creator turned the disobedient people into the first bison herd; bison later provide food, tools, clothing, shelter, water; bison hoofprints led the people. Same NPS source. Handle respectfully; flag as culturally weighty, not as a generic animal enemy.
- **Great spotted eagle (Wanblee Galeshka)** rescues a girl after the flood and they have twins (Lame Deer, https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/unktehi-and-the-flood-as-told-by-lame-deer.htm). Flag: eagle is sacred in the sources' wider context; it appears in a story that also explains the sacred pipestone. Do not use as a creature that fights or can be hurt.
- **Boy and the Bear**: a bear woman raises a boy among the people and grieves at his father's scaffold. https://www.wolakotaproject.org/storytelling-with-duane-hollow-horn-bear/ . Burial-scaffold content is mortuary; treat cautiously.
- **Badger family and the bear cub** (Dakota, Zitkala-Sa) as above.

## Hoarding/swallowing monster motifs (for "released, not taken" rewards)

- Iya the eater: per a second-hand summary, a swallowed Iktomi cuts Iya open and frees everyone. Unverified; read the Deloria text. https://muse.jhu.edu/article/531633/summary (title) .
- Bear who keeps a family's dried meat and arrows, then is driven off while the cub shares food back: motif is hoarding, ending is return and sharing, no kill. https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/zitkala-sa/old-indian-legends/text/single-page (Dakota).
- Anog-Ite lures people up then leaves them unprepared (a trap by deception, not swallowing); people are released by the Creator's help, back down through the cave. https://www.nps.gov/wica/learn/historyculture/the-lakota-emergence-story.htm . Sacred-adjacent; do not make her a boss.
- Iktomi traps prairie chickens in a hut; Coyote then steals the cooked birds. Trickster hoarding backfires. https://www.wolakotaproject.org/storytelling-with-duane-hollow-horn-bear/

## Clothing by gender (everyday, not ceremonial regalia)

- Women: mid-shin dresses of elk, deer or buffalo hide, knee-high leggings, moccasins. https://www.ndstudies.gov/curriculum/high-school/standing-rock-oyate/culture-standing-rock (corroborate). A museum note says dresses were traditionally of two deer or elk skins, hind section folded over to form a yoke (search summary of a museum record; not opened).
- Men: leather breechcloth in summer; buckskin leggings and shirts in winter. Same Standing Rock source.
- Both: buffalo robes in winter. Same source.
- Decoration: porcupine quills, plant and berry paints; glass seed beads adopted from the early 1800s. https://aktalakota.stjo.org/lakota-culture/beliefs-traditions/native-american-clothing/
- Moccasins differed by cut, lining, sole, quillwork, beadwork and painted designs, so tribal affiliation could be read from them. Same Akta Lakota source.
- Children: mothers and grandmothers made decorated moccasins, shirts, vests, dresses and pants, but the page ties these to ceremonies and special occasions, not everyday wear. Same source.
- Gap: I found no source for everyday children's clothing, or for plain (undecorated) work clothes. Large beaded dresses (three elk skins, 13 to 16 pounds) are reservation-era and showpiece, not everyday; skip.

## Tools and weapons

- Bow and arrow was the main hunting tool; a short bow is noted for fast close-range shooting (search summary of a Lakota museum collection page; weak). Before horses, hunting buffalo on foot with a bow was hard. https://www.ndstudies.gov/gr8/node/245 (not opened).
- Buffalo bones became knives, arrowheads, scrapers, awls; horns became spoons, cups, ladles; buffalo chips fuel. https://www.ndstudies.gov/curriculum/high-school/standing-rock-oyate/culture-standing-rock
- Women's hide work: elk-horn scraper (wahintke in the summary; spelling unverified) thins and cleans hides; bone awls; parfleche (rawhide cases) for clothing, food, other goods; travois from tipi poles on a horse harness; women made tipis of buffalo hide (small tipi seven or eight hides). https://algae-aardvark-stp6.squarespace.com/museum-articles/elk-horn-scrapers (search summary; museum blog; weak) and the Standing Rock source for tipis.
- Gap: no source for clubs, shields, lances, or digging sticks; weapons for fighting characters need a better source.

## Fighting traditions (e.g. counting coup, mounted/foot fighting, defensive skills) — factual

- Counting coup (striking an enemy with a coup stick or bare hand) earned more respect than killing. https://www.ndstudies.gov/curriculum/high-school/standing-rock-oyate/culture-standing-rock ; also https://plainshumanities.unl.edu/encyclopedia/doc/egp.war.052.html (Plains-wide, search summary). Coup systems were tribe-specific (USC Kama Kapuska project, search summary only). Fits the "no killing, no trophies" brief; note it also existed within a warrior-honour system, so avoid stacking rewards.
- Akicita: men deputised to attend to civil affairs such as keeping order in camp, organising the buffalo hunt and breaking camp; chosen for generosity, leadership and bravery. https://core.ac.uk/download/237301084.pdf (1900s ethnographic text, search summary) . Corroborate with a Lakota source.
- Hunting on foot before horses; horses later central (see tools). Details of mounted fighting I could not source.
- In story: skunk's defence ends a battle (Hollow Horn Bear, https://www.wolakotaproject.org/storytelling-with-duane-hollow-horn-bear/) , a defensive-skill motif that is not sacred.
- Gap: no Lakota-authored source on fighting skills, formations, or non-lethal defence.

## Restricted or sacred — do NOT use in game (list with reason and source)

- Sacred pipe and pipestone: the pipe is sacred ("comes alive when used in a ceremony"); red pipestone is tied to the ancestors' blood and flesh. https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/unktehi-and-the-flood-as-told-by-lame-deer.htm . Also covered by CULTURE.md.
- Unktehi, the horned water monster: the story ties her to the origin of the pipestone; she was punished and turned to stone; the flood story is entangled with sacred origin. Not shown defeated by people. Same source. Excluded as a foe.
- Anog-Ite (double-faced woman), Iktomi's partner in the emergence story, and the Creator and Tokahe: parts of the Lakota emergence story, told by a tribal historian and spiritual leader, with a "portal to the spirit world" said to be hidden in the cave. https://www.nps.gov/wica/learn/historyculture/the-lakota-emergence-story.htm . Treat the whole emergence story and the Wind Cave interior as sacred; stay out of it unless the user decides otherwise.
- Eagle (Wanblee Galeshka), eagle feathers and headdresses: CULTURE.md bars them as loot. Eagle also sacred per Lame Deer's story. Same sources.
- Black Hills (Pahá Sápa / He Sapa) and the sacred fire (Péta wakaŋ): called sacred by Oglala leaders and Akta Lakota. https://www.wakpamnilake-nsn.gov/ ; https://aktalakota.stjo.org/oceti-sakowin-seven-council-fires/ . No ceremony, fire-keeping or pipe as a mechanic.
- Buffalo and scaffolds: Lakota creation links buffalo to the people, and "The Boy and the Bear" includes a funeral scaffold. https://www.wolakotaproject.org/storytelling-with-duane-hollow-horn-bear/ . Do not hurt bison or show burial scaffolds.
- Story on prayers before the pipe (How the People Prayed Before the Pipe, told by Joe Eagle Elk) and grief/history stories (1889 ration-point gambling and alcohol story, "A Proper Grieving"): about prayer, trauma and alcohol; excluded by CULTURE.md ("no alcohol stereotypes") and sacredness. Same We Lakota source.
- Iya: sources I read give no indication either way whether Iya is sacred today. Excluded until the user confirms.
