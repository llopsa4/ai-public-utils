## Homeland and landscapes (for home-region rooms)

- The Lower Sioux community says its members are Mdewakanton Dakota and the traditional Minnesota River Valley homeland was called Cansa'yapi, "where they marked the trees red"; Minnesota is "the place where the water reflects the sky". https://lowersioux.com/about-us/
- Before 1862 four Dakota bands lived along the Minnesota River: Mdewakanton and Wahpekute ("lower bands"), Sisseton and Wahpeton ("upper bands"). https://lowersioux.com/about-us/
- The Shakopee community describes Dakota life in the Minnesota River Valley: fishing rivers, hunting on prairies and in river-valley woodlands. https://shakopeedakota.org/culture-history/our-history/ (timeline's own wording)
- Bdote, "where two waters come together" (Minnesota and Mississippi rivers), is described as a centre of Dakota spirituality and history and the Mdewakanton place of origin. https://www.mnhs.org/usdakotawar/stories/history/dakota-homeland-land-lifestyle/bdote (corroborating, non-Dakota host). Bdote is a place of origin: treat as sacred geography; at most mention, do not make a dungeon or arena.
- Landscape cues in Zitkala-Sa's stories: lake with reeds, mud, creek, sage bush, willow, tree with bark, buffalo, deer, wolves, coyote, prairie camps of teepees. https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/zitkala-sa/old-indian-legends/text/single-page
- Bison herd (pteoptaye) returned to SMSC in 2023 as a gift from the Sisseton Wahpeton Oyate. https://shakopeedakota.org/culture-history/our-history/
- (round 2) Sisseton-Wahpeton Oyate (own site): its territory is the Lake Traverse Reservation in northeastern South Dakota and southeastern North Dakota; seven districts with Dakota names and English glosses (e.g. Iyakaptapi, "Big Coulee"); members are the Sisitunwan-Wahpetunwan Oyate. The page says little else about lifeways. https://swo-nsn.gov/?p=2821 . The 1867 treaty boundaries cite Lake Traverse, Lake Kampeska and the Coteau des Prairies (South Dakota Magazine, non-nation, via search summary: https://southdakotamagazine.com/sisseton-peace-medallion).
- (round 2) Landscape from Eastman (Santee Dakota author, source 13): woods, lakes and rivers (Minnesota and Mississippi); Lakes Calhoun and Harriet where Cloud Man's village stood; spring maple-sugar groves of maple and birch with birch-bark sap troughs; midsummer gatherings on a strip between lake and river; autumn duck and deer hunting on a wild-rice lake, August wild rice harvest; winter cold, cracking trees, ice bursting "like thunder", blizzards and prairie fires; March thaw and snow crust.
- (round 2) Which community to follow: nation sources describe homelands as follows. Lower Sioux and Shakopee (Mdewakanton): Minnesota River Valley and Bdote, river-valley woods and prairies, rivers fished (sources above). Sisseton-Wahpeton: Lake Traverse and Coteau des Prairies country, prairie lake region. Eastman's lake, river, maple-sugar and wild-rice scenery matches the Minnesota (Mdewakanton/Santee) side. Nation sources do not say which a non-member game should choose; that is the user's decision (Open question 6). Choosing the Minnesota River valley avoids Bdote itself if the rooms use generic woods, lakes, river and prairie.
- Classification note: Sources found do not themselves discuss Yankton-Yanktonai classification disputes. Zitkala-Sa is described as Yankton Dakota (source 2). The Lakota/Dakota/Nakota split by the user's brief is accepted as given; no dispute documented here.

## Publicly told stories with defeatable monsters (story, monster, how it is defeated, whether publicly told/published; ONLY candidates clearly NOT sacred beings honoured today)

All from Zitkala-Sa, Old Indian Legends (published 1901): https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/zitkala-sa/old-indian-legends/text/single-page

1. **Iya, the Camp-Eater.** Monster: Iya, a giant who disguises himself as a baby and eats a camp from within. Defeat: a young mother notices the sounds; the village flees at midnight; at dawn Iya chases them but his thin legs fail, and warriors with long knives kill him. Released: the whole tribe emerges from his body with their camp ("We are glad to be free!"). Published: yes. Sacred? Nothing found marking Iya as honoured; but Iya also appears in Deloria's "Iktomi conquers Iya, the eater" (catalogue title only, text not read), so check with Dakota speakers.
2. **Manstin, the Rabbit (Double-Face).** Monster: Double-Face, a giant with a face front and back who tortures a stolen baby with a thorny rose bush. Defeat: Manstin shoots him from behind a sage bush with a poisoned arrow. Freed: the baby, raised to a man. Published: yes. Sacred? Nothing found.
3. **The Warlike Seven (Iya, the Eater, as drinker of lakes).** Iya drinks the lake and swallows Iktomi; Iktomi cuts his way out with a knife; the water pours out. Released: Fish, Turtle and Iktomi. Published: yes. Note: the story's ending drowns villagers; design caution.
4. **Shooting of the Red Eagle / The Tree-Bound.** Monster: a giant red eagle that arrows cannot hit; a young avenger kills it with a poisoned flint arrow. DOUBTFUL: eagles are honoured (see Restricted). It is a monster in the story, but the user should decide if a giant "red eagle" is acceptable. Recommend skip.
5. **The Badger and the Bear.** Antagonist: a black bear who takes the badgers' den and food; a magic-arrow avenger (born from buffalo blood the badger prayed over) confronts him and the bears flee. A bear is an animal relative, not a monster; not recommended as a foe.

Not candidates:
- **Unktehi (horned water monster).** In the Lame Deer account (Lakota teller, from a Santee grandmother) she is not defeated by the people but turned to stone, her bones in the Badlands. https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/unktehi-and-the-flood-as-told-by-lame-deer.htm . Status as sacred vs monster is unresolved: leave out.
- **Iktomi** is a trickster in many tales; his tales are public, but he is a recurring character, not a monster (see Helper section).

### Iya and Double-Face: status findings (round 2)

- **Iya: unresolved, leaning "not confirmed safe".** No Dakota- or Lakota-authored statement on whether Iya is honoured today was found. Zitkala-Sa's own preface says "Iktomi, the snare weaver, Iya, the Eater, and Old Double-Face are not wholly fanciful creatures", which treats them as more than invention but does not call them sacred or evil (https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/zitkala-sa/old-indian-legends/text/preface). Deloria's Dakota Texts opens with "Iktomi conquers Iya, the eater" (Dakota author, catalogue title only: https://openlibrary.org/books/OL3419587M); Julian Rice (1984, non-Dakota scholar, summary only) says Iktomi destroys Iya as a rival predator, i.e. Iya is shown defeated in Deloria's collection too (https://muse.jhu.edu/article/531633/summary). Search results conflict on sacred versus evil: some sites call Iya sacred to the Lakota, others "utterly evil", the latter traced to James R. Walker (non-Lakota collector); none by a Lakota or Dakota author. Riggs entry for "iya": not found (PDF fetch 403). Treat Iya as a story monster shown defeated by two independent Dakota-authored collections, but unconfirmed by any living-nation source.
- **Double-Face vs Anog-Ite: not confirmed related, not confirmed unrelated.** Zitkala-Sa's "Old Double-Face" is a giant with a face front and back, named in the same preface list (source above). The Oglala Lakota emergence story (NPS) has Anog-Ite, "double face woman" who "had two faces on her head", a banished former wife of the wind spirit who deceives the first people; the page does not call her sacred but she belongs to the emergence story. https://www.nps.gov/wica/learn/historyculture/the-lakota-emergence-story.htm . No source found linking the two or stating they are the same; the shared "double face" motif is the only link. Keep Anog-Ite off-limits (emergence story); Double-Face stays a candidate only with user approval.

## Games, training, fighting traditions (round 2, Eastman, Indian Boyhood, source 13; fetch-extracted)

- Training: boys taught silence, patience, self-control, to rise before dawn; tested by an uncle with sudden noises at night; sent alone at night for water; learned bird names, tracking, noticing nature; repeated legends nightly; respect for elders and generosity to the poor. Boys earned names through feats.
- Games: off-hand arrow shooting (speed and height of a leading arrow), foot and pony races, swimming, lacrosse (a large contest between bands) and shinny, wrestling, mud-and-willow fights, tops, snow-arrows, throwing wands, lasso practice, chipmunk hunts in March. These are the safest "fighting style" sources: skill games, tracking, archery.
- Hunting wisdom: do not approach dangerous animals head-on; hunts announced and policed, no man hunts alone in the herd; wolves attack in packs, a lone wolf will not attack a man. Women read footprints and built stockades for defence.
- Do NOT use: coup counting, scalp marks, war-bonnets for battles, war-whoop (CULTURE.md forbids war-whoop sounds), visions before war, Bear Dance and medicine-dance play (ceremonial), the "Great Mystery" offering. Listed so design knows what to avoid.

### 1. Monsters and hostile beings (clean ones are few)

Old Indian Legends has no monster beyond the three known (14 stories read: https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/zitkala-sa/old-indian-legends/text/single-page ). Wigwam Evenings has 20 tales; only two give a clean story monster:
- **Eya the Devourer** (Eastman, 12th evening, https://childlit.unl.edu/eas1912.2010.001.html). A second Dakota-authored telling of the approved Iya (spelled Eya). A found baby is never satisfied; when he screams, swallowed people are seen in his throat; the camp flees before dawn. Unktomee (Iktomi) meets them and tricks Eya into swallowing mussel shells he takes for dried human ears; the men cut him open with knives. Released: the swallowed company pours out onto the plain, singing and dancing in thanks. Usable as a variant boss mechanic (a decoy leaves him helpless): keep shells, drop the "ears". Sacred status: same unresolved note as above; the user's 2026-11-18 community check covers the Dakota Camp-Eater.
- **Double-Face in "The Comrades"** (Eastman, 20th evening, same link). Tortures children and lures them away; Mashtinna the Rabbit shoots him through the heart; the baby boy is freed and grows to a youth. A second independent telling of the Zitkala-Sa story; the summary names no link to Anog-Ite.
- **Driven off, not killed (doubtful; animals are relatives):** the stingy Bear who drives the Badger family from their den; an Avenger drives him away and the youngest cub feeds the badgers (Eastman 7th evening; Zitkala-Sa too). The Crane whose hunts on the frogs are each broken up (Eastman 2nd evening). Winged and crawling attackers driven off by fire in "The First Battle" (Eastman 16th evening): part of the Little Boy Man creation cycle, so inspiration only for the approved invented swarm enemies.
- **Not recommended, sacred or contested:** Unk-tay-hee and his mate in "The Little Boy Man" (the water monster is speared and sinks, but the story includes He-who-was-first-Created, the Sun and sweat-lodge steam; same Unktehi as the contested entry above). Wa-kee-yan (Thunder-Bird, "servant of the Great Mystery") and Wa-zee-yah (Spirit of Cold, shown as purifying, not hostile, so no Dakota support for a Cold foe).
- Not monsters: Iktomi drowned by Patkasa (Zitkala-Sa; trickster, Open question 11); the Field-Mouse that kills the Buffalo (avoid: bison); the Wildcat in "Dance in a Buffalo Skull" (not defeated); the old father-in-law outwitted in "The Son-in-Law" (a human). In Indian Boyhood the only "defeats" are a grizzly killed by two named war chiefs and fights with named human enemies: no.
- Deloria Dakota Texts titles seen in catalogues (text unread, nothing claimed): Iktomi conquers Iya, Doubleface tricks a girl, Turtle-Moccasin Boy, Blood-Clot Boy, Eagle Boy, White-Plume Boy (NYPL link above). Defeated monster in them: not found.

### 3. Men's everyday clothing, tools and equipment
Sources: Indian Boyhood (https://dn721501.ca.archive.org/0/items/indianboyhood00337gut/indbo10.txt ), Indian Child Life (link above), Zitkala-Sa (Standard Ebooks link above).
- Fringed leather leggings, beaded moccasins, fringed buckskin shirt, buffalo robe hair-inside with leather belt (already approved). An otter-skin hair tie appears as everyday in Eastman, but otter-skin head-dresses are war dress: avoid both to be safe.
- Bow and arrows (blunt-headed for small game), fringed quiver, belt knife in a beaded case, stone knives and hatchets "in the old days", stone and short-handled axes, rawhide ropes and lariats, horn spoon, willow walking sticks (Zitkala-Sa).
- Two snowshoe kinds, a long straight-toed pair and a shorter upturned-toed pair; sleds of buffalo ribs; basswood-bark skates and coasting strips (Indian Boyhood).
- Lacrosse: netted sticks, field about three-quarters of a mile (Indian Child Life, "A Midsummer Feast"). The balls were made by a medicine-man (red and black): treat the ball as plain, never show its making.
- Willow fish basket; fishing line of wild hemp, sinew or horse-hair; fish speared or shot with arrows; horse-hair snare on a bent sapling; a birchen doe-caller (Indian Boyhood, "The Boy Hunter").
- Carrying: dog and pony travois of rawhide strips lashed to tent-poles; water bags of tripe or pericardium; rawhide cases (parfleches) for rice; dried-meat bags (Zitkala-Sa). A carrying strap as such: not found. Nearest sourced items: the wide leather belt and the travois rawhide strips.
- Harvest tools: birch canoe, a light rod to strike grain into the canoe, digging sticks for roots, a wooden paddle to test syrup on snow, birch-bark pans, basswood troughs, brass kettles (Indian Boyhood).
- Do not use: red face paint and "a red feather in the hair" (Zitkala-Sa), feathers, war-lances, war dress, the filled pipe, council sticks, poisoned arrows, kinnikinick for smoking.

### 4. Seasonal places and activities for rooms
- **Maple-sugar camp, March thaw to April:** bark sugar house in a maple grove on the Minnesota River, sap logs hollowed like canoes, trough and birch basin, women test trees with an axe, a hardwood chip drives sap into a dish, each boy tends one kettle and tests syrup on snow with a paddle; rabbits and squirrels gnaw the birch pans. Indian Boyhood Ch. IV (mirror above; same text https://scienceviews.com/indian/indianboyhood3.html ). Nation-authored corroboration: Hocokata Ti, sap mid-March to mid-April, axe, trough, birch-bark basket, large carved log; no ceremony named (https://hocokatati.org/?p=25929).
- **Wild-rice lake, August:** 15 to 20 families at a lake, teepees on high ground but life largely in birch canoes; grain tied in bundles, two per canoe (one paddles, one strikes the heads), dried on robes and mats, parched, hulled by treading in a pit, winnowed on a robe, cached in grass-lined holes. Indian Boyhood, wild-harvest chapter (mirror above, offset 200000). Blueberry picking comes first. Skip the offering to the Water Chief and the dancing in the pits.
- **Winter lodge:** buffalo-skin teepee heated with logs, snowshoes, hunting on the snow crust, dog sleds with buffalo-rib runners, a blizzard shelter under the snow (Indian Boyhood); a winter teepee camp by a frozen lake with wood gathering and sledding (Wigwam Evenings). Eastman's "Winter Camp" is on the Mouse river (Plains), not Minnesota: use generic scenery only.
- **Fishing brook:** brooks dammed to drive fish into willow baskets; fish tickled out in fall (Indian Boyhood, "The Boy Hunter").
- **River crossing:** buffalo-skin round boats on the Missouri, and a grandmother swimming a swift stream with a boy on her back (Indian Boyhood, "Early Hardships", "My Indian Grandmother"). Plains setting: use only a generic ford or swim.
- **Autumn lake hunt:** ducks "in countless numbers", deer hunted by torchlight from canoes (Indian Boyhood, "Adventures of My Uncle"). **Summer:** meat dried on willow racks (Zitkala-Sa); a midsummer feast with lacrosse (Indian Child Life; naming rites there: skip).
- Seasonal round (non-nation summary): spring sugar camps, rice in early fall, then the deer hunt (search summary, likely the MNHS page https://www.mnhs.org/fortsnelling/learn/native-americans/dakota-people , not fetched).

### 5. Foods and goods for "land renews" rewards (no medicine, no ceremony)
- Maple sugar cakes moulded in birch forms into reeds and duck and goose bills (art-friendly); white box-elder sugar (Indian Boyhood Ch. IV). Birch and ash dark bitter sugar was medicinal: skip.
- Wild rice (see flag below), teepsinna (wild turnip), wild sweet potatoes, blueberries, huckleberries, wild cherries pounded into dried cakes (Indian Boyhood, wild-harvest chapter).
- Dried and pounded meat, dried venison, parched corn, fish, duck (Indian Boyhood, Wigwam Evenings); rice with venison, rice sprinkled with maple sugar (Wigwam Evenings).
- Goods: birch canoe, rawhide rice case, fish basket, mat. A "renew the land" act such as re-seeding or leaving a share: not found in any source; that framing would be the designer's invention.
- Skip manakcahkcah (wild lily root, "medicine and food") and honey raids (trickster-story detail).
